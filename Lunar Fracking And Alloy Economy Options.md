# Lunar Fracking And Alloy Economy Options

Source: AFK agent analysis
Imported: 2026-10-10

## Source Snapshot

> Current-use note, 2026-10-23: Review Crater Nodes Proposal and Design Lunar Crater Processing Recipe Chain supersede the interruption, Scandium and numerical economy assumptions below. Native GT failure is required. Finite reserve and fracking history advance only on successful completion. Preserve this report as the original comparison, not current balance.

This is an economic comparison for owner redlining. It is not accepted balance, a scientific material specification, an implementation plan, or a playable-pack verification. Every new number is a fictional balance example unless identified as an existing recipe. The report deliberately does not select scandium, a final alloy composition, or a new technology gate.

### Recommendation

Keep a usable iron-based bulk route. Make the better production bands recover more useful construction material from that bulk feed, rather than asking the player to build infrastructure mostly out of gold or platinum. Report bulk-feed utilization, scarce additives, electricity, processing time and transported inputs separately. One number cannot honestly rank all of those costs.

Choose the crater deterioration curve in terms of fresh carrier demand after recovery. Then derive gross input from that demand. This makes a supposedly gentle curve mean something to the player's actual supply line. Prefer a slowly increasing, unbounded curve over an artificial operation cap. A logarithmic candidate is illustrated below, not selected.

### Accepted Boundaries Used

- A crater has one finite ordinary reserve and a subsequent unlimited fracking history shared by any number of extractors.
- Ordinary mining happens first. Fluid does not allow skipping that reserve.
- Each fracking operation has fixed enriched-fluid output while its gross fluid input rises with shared history.
- Recovery is partial. Fresh carrier remains necessary.
- Deposits use normal GT materials. No lunar-provenance iron, gold or other raw-item variants are proposed.
- Iron, platinum, gold, cobalt and aluminium are available content candidates in this discussion. Scandium is not approved.
- One canonical Space-Grade Alloy is a construction material, not the material that gates technology tiers.
- Normal GT refining requirements remain. No reverse inference from a normal metal ingot to an ore count is made here.
- Downstream machinery follows normal GT recipe maps. Few useful intermediates are preferable to a custom machine per process.
- The provisional factory bootstrap is charged ordinary GT batteries and a suitable Battery Buffer. Combustion generation is banned in space, including an oxygenated Station room. New Space Grade Solar Panel items are not a power source.

### What Efficiency Should Mean

| Metric | Useful meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk-feed utilization | Finished alloy material units divided by the bulk alloy-forming feed units | That gold and iron have equal acquisition cost |
| All alloy-forming input utilization | Finished alloy units divided by bulk feed plus consumed metallic or metalloid additives | Real kilogram conservation across unlike GT materials |
| Scarce reagent intensity | Each Au, Co, Pt or other scarce reagent unit consumed per finished alloy unit | A universal combined price for those reagents |
| Carrier intensity | Fresh mB after cleanup per extraction batch or per finished alloy unit | Equal mineral content for different enriched-fluid variants |
| Electricity intensity | Measured EU through every required operation per finished unit | Low peak demand or a sufficient battery inventory |
| Processing intensity | Machine-seconds per finished unit and line bottleneck | Wall-clock time when unlimited machines are assumed |
| Capital and freight | Machines, installed power, buffered working stock and imported inputs | A permanent recipe ingredient or technology gate |

For fictional recipe comparisons, one full elemental GT dust or ingot unit can be a nominal alloy-forming material unit. Tiny dust is one ninth of that unit where that form is actually available. Silicon is included as alloy-forming feed despite being a metalloid. Compound dust must first be translated through its actual retained refining route. Hematite dust is not an iron ingot. Bauxite dust is not an aluminium ingot. Counting either as finished metal would conceal refining costs and gates.

These nominal units are game recipe bookkeeping, not kilograms. Equal item counts of Fe, Au, Co and Si do not prove equal physical mass. A scientific mass claim would need actual composition and atomic or material mass accounting. The fictional canonical alloy can have several functional feedstock specifications, but the report does not claim those are physically identical real alloys.

Compare complete loops. A recovery step that consumes fresh platinum must include that platinum. A returned intermediate must not be counted as both finished output and reclaimed feed. Energy or scarce-metal savings must not be inferred solely from the larger final ingot stack.

### Concrete Three-Band Yield Schedule

The coordinated recipe-draft candidate uses a common iron/silicon base and one common useful alloy dross. It avoids separate provenance or quality grades. All chemistry and quantities below are proposals. This is the economic accounting of the candidate, not evidence that the chemistry is real or available in the pack.

| Band | Complete steady-state accounting batch | Finished SGA | Bulk utilization | Utilization including metallic additives |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Simple | 10 Fe + 2 Si produces 6 SGA and 6 common dross, with dross discarded or stockpiled | 6 | 50% | 50% |
| Medium | Two base batches supply 24 bulk units and 12 dross. Three recovery operations each consume 4 dross, 1 carbon, 1 tiny Au and 1 tiny Co and return 2 additional SGA | 18 | 75% | 72.97% |
| Advanced | Ten base batches supply 120 bulk units and 60 dross. Twelve operations each consume 5 dross, 250 mB HCl and 1 tiny Pt. Their combined solution yields 48 additional SGA through normal processing | 108 | 90% | 89.01% |

Medium metallic input is 24 + 3/9 Au + 3/9 Co = 24.6667 units. Its honest all-input utilization is 18/24.6667. Advanced input is 120 + 12/9 Pt = 121.3333 units, giving 108/121.3333. Carbon and acid remain separate consumed-reagent axes. They are not silently treated as incorporated bulk metal.

| Band | Bulk feed per 1,000 SGA | Scarce reagent per 1,000 SGA | Other recovery reagent per 1,000 SGA |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Simple | 2,000 nominal units | None in this recovery schedule | None |
| Medium | 1,333.33 nominal units | 18.52 Au and 18.52 Co units | 166.67 carbon units |
| Advanced | 1,111.11 nominal units | 12.35 Pt units | 27,777.78 mB HCl |

These are long-run ratios. Actual crafting rounds up whole recipes and leaves reusable stock. Medium's worked batch is exactly two base batches and three recovery operations. Advanced's is ten base batches, twelve leaches and the matching whole solution-processing batches. Do not force the base recipe to have those large output sizes just to make the accounting align.

The simple route remains valuable when Fe is easy, batteries are scarce, or transport of additives is inconvenient. Medium and advanced reduce bulk feed but consume other resources and machine capacity. Their exact electricity and duration are not established here. A recipe with a better material ratio is not automatically cheaper overall.

For comparison, an earlier major-fraction alternative used Au and Co inside a different bulk mix. Even a reduced medium example required about 0.222 Co unit per SGA. That is twelve times the 0.01852 Co unit in the common-base recovery candidate. The major-fraction recipe can be a local-surplus alternative, but should not be labelled globally cheap infrastructure without comparing actual local supplies. If aluminium is used in a later alternative, its ordinary refining and smelting requirements remain in the total bill.

Scandium is unnecessary for this numerical ladder. Adding it would need a demonstrated material role and an accessible retained production chain. An obscure element should not be selected merely to make the advanced column look advanced.

### Opportunity Cost Of Rare Inputs

A rare additive has an opportunity cost even when a player already owns it. Using Au or Pt for bulk alloy means that stock is unavailable for other equipment or sale. Local surplus can reduce that cost, but a high utilization percentage does not erase it. There is no supplied verified default scandium acquisition route, so scandium must not be priced as a free bauxite byproduct. The advanced Pt recovery option is non-Sc, but still needs a verified retained Pt supply chain.

Let B be the player's acquisition cost of one nominal bulk feed unit with the proposed 10 Fe to 2 Si ratio. Let A, C and P be the acquisition costs of one Au, Co and Pt unit. These are comparative values, not a new game currency. Before energy, freight and extra machinery, the candidate costs per SGA are:

- Simple: 2B
- Medium: 4B/3 + A/54 + C/54 + one sixth of the cost of a carbon unit
- Advanced: 10B/9 + P/81 + the cost of 27.78 mB HCl

Ignoring its carbon and added processing costs, medium only beats simple when A + C is less than 36B. If Au costs 30B and Co costs 6B, the saved bulk feed exactly pays for those two additives. Added carbon, electricity and machinery then make medium more expensive. These are fictional shadow prices to demonstrate the comparison, not assigned values for GT materials.

Against simple, advanced saves 8B/9 of bulk feed per SGA. Before acid and processing, its Pt therefore must cost less than 72B per full unit to preserve a cost advantage. If Pt costs 100B, its 1/81-unit dose already exceeds that bulk saving. The 90% bulk-utilization route is then more expensive, despite needing less iron and silicon. Compare advanced with medium using both routes' actual reagent bills, not merely 90% versus 75%.

The economic recommendation is consequently a choice among usable routes, not an assertion that every factory should run the highest band. Keep the common-metal route viable and preserve the normal processing requirements for all optional metals.

### No Selectable Precious-Metal Recycling

Canonical SGA and common unidentified dross must never decompose into a player's choice of Au, Co, Pt or Sc. The iron route can create the same canonical SGA and dross, so such decomposition would turn common feed into precious metal. Different authored feedstock recipes do not justify a selectable reverse recipe when origin is deliberately not retained.

In this candidate, dross recovery returns only canonical SGA material. Consumed promoters have no returned precious-metal output. The accounted recoverable dross is bounded by the lost bulk feed from its producing recipe. Ordinary SGA remelting or remanufacture must also preserve or reduce that material quantity, rather than creating more recoverable dross plus a full original alloy output.

If a separately identified standard GT reagent produces a recoverable standard GT byproduct under its unchanged processing chain, that belongs to that actual known-input recipe. It is not evidence for precious-metal extraction from canonical alloy or unidentified common dross. No reverse ore-refining recipe is required here.

### Three Carrier-Recovery Alternatives

These are mutually comparable balance settings, not three automatically approved technology tiers. Enriched output remains 1,000 mB per operation. For comparison, each alternative assumes the draft separation output of 800 mB spent carrier and an optional cleanup using two operations' spent fluid. No extra recoverable fluid scales with crater age.

| Alternative | Gross starting input | Carrier returned per operation | Fresh makeup at start | Cleanup output from 1,600 mB spent | Fresh makeup when gross input reaches 1,250 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Lower recovery | 1,000 mB | 500 mB | 500 mB | 1,000 mB | 750 mB |
| Intermediate recovery | 1,000 mB | 650 mB | 350 mB | 1,300 mB | 600 mB |
| Draft high recovery | 1,000 mB | 750 mB | 250 mB | 1,500 mB | 500 mB |

The draft's 1,000 initial input and 750 return are not accepted quantities. They are a useful high-recovery example. A 25% rise in its gross input doubles fresh makeup. With 500 recovery, the same gross rise increases makeup by only 50%. Therefore neither a percentage change in gross input nor a percentage recovery label is enough to describe deterioration.

At each step, fresh makeup is q(n) minus fixed recovery r. A chosen schedule must keep q(n) greater than r, including rounding, so full recycling never removes fresh demand. Spent-carrier cleanup must not generate more volume than the defined spent batch unless an explicit consumed additional input accounts for it.

With no cleanup, the player imports or makes the whole q(n). With cleanup, they import or make q(n) minus r and pay cleanup's energy and capacity costs. That is a genuine choice, rather than a mandatory intermediate with no alternative.

### Cost Curves Based On Net Makeup

Let n be the zero-based number of already claimed fracking operations on the crater. Let r be fixed recoverable carrier. Let F0 be initial fresh demand after complete cleanup. Then q(n) = r + F0 times g(n/N). N is a production-volume horizon, not a time limit or crater capacity.

The following fictional comparison uses r = 750 mB, F0 = 250 mB and N = 10,000 operations. Each curve doubles fresh demand at n = N. Every operation still outputs the same 1,000 mB enriched fluid and the same authored resource batch.

| Curve | g(x) | Fresh demand at 0 / 10,000 / 100,000 operations | Practical effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linear | 1 + x | 250 / 500 / 2,750 mB | Easy to predict, strongest late relocation pressure |
| Square-root | 1 + (sqrt(1 + x) - 1) / (sqrt(2) - 1) | 250 / 500 / 1,648.2 mB | Increasing burden with a gentler late slope |
| Logarithmic | 1 + log2(1 + x) | 250 / 500 / 1,114.9 mB | Long-lived sites remain viable, weakest late relocation pressure |

| Shared fracking count n | Linear gross input | Square-root gross input | Logarithmic gross input |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| 1,000 | 1,025 | 1,029.5 | 1,034.4 |
| 10,000 | 1,250 | 1,250 | 1,250 |
| 50,000 | 2,250 | 1,874.8 | 1,646.2 |
| 100,000 | 3,500 | 2,398.2 | 1,864.9 |

All quantities are mB per operation. Tables use continuous values for comparison. A final integer rule should preserve monotonicity and positive makeup without creating batches that get an artificially cheap rate. Rounding policy is not chosen here.

The logarithmic candidate is the most conservative recommendation for the requested slow rise. It is unbounded, so it eventually creates a supply burden, but there is no arbitrary extraction cutoff. It may be too gentle where water and lubricant are produced locally and piping is cheap. That is a real tradeoff, not a reason to invent a hidden cap.

The illustrative N = 10,000 is not a recommended accepted value. At a fictional 30-second base operation, reaching it takes 83.33 ideal hours with one extractor, 10.42 hours with eight, or 1.30 hours with 64. These ignore processing, power and input bottlenecks. More machines should age a shared crater faster in wall-clock time because they extract more total resources. A counter based on active machines or individual drill uptime would distort this accounting.

Cleanup saves 750 mB fresh carrier per operation, but its 9,000 EU cost exceeds the 144 Mixer EU avoided. Cleanup is a carrier and freight saving, not a demonstrated electricity saving. Lubricant manufacture and transport may change the complete economic comparison. Do not declare those savings without measuring them.

Without cleanup, extraction plus separation is 120,000 EU per operation and initial fresh Mixer manufacture adds 192 EU. With cleanup it is 129,048 EU. This example makes the choice especially relevant during the battery bootstrap. It does not establish that cleanup is bad, because freight and reagent access are separate costs.

One shared Centrifuge doing both separation and cleanup needs 20 + 15 = 35 machine-seconds per batch. That cannot indefinitely keep up with a 30-second extractor. Buffering postpones this bottleneck, but does not remove it. Separate separation and cleanup machines can maintain the illustrated single-extractor rate at base speed, subject to actual recipe execution and power supply. Larger operations need proportional downstream capacity or validated overclocking.

For a continuously supplied ideal line, extraction averages 120 EU/t, separation 80 EU/t and cleanup 15 EU/t, totaling 215 EU/t before refining. If all three machines run together, their combined draw is 270 EU/t. Average draw and peak draw are different battery-buffer requirements. Actual tier and amperage behavior needs source or pack verification.

Use actual transported usable EU to size the battery shipment. As a purely fictional illustration, 1 million usable EU funds at most seven of the 129,000-EU extraction/separation/cleanup batches. Eight million funds at most 62. Neither number claims an actual battery's capacity. Metal refining and alloying reduce those counts, as do electrical losses and overclocking. Surface-charged batteries remain the provisional bootstrap. A Space Grade Solar Panel component does not make this plant self-powered, and combustion is not an exception.

### Shared History, Batching And Relocation

For a batch of k fracking operations starting after n already claimed operations, charge the sum q(n) through q(n + k - 1). Return k fixed enriched batches and, after whole cleanup batches, at most k times the fixed recovery. Charging k times q(n) would let larger batches bypass deterioration. Concurrent extractors must receive successive shared positions, not all read the same cheap next position.

Normal interruption resumes the already claimed operation. Replacing a machine must not reset crater history. Abandoned started work loses its committed inputs under the accepted shared-operation simplification. Failed attempts before an operation claims work should not create product or advance a free product cycle. This report does not prescribe synchronization or persistence implementation.

Relocation should be an economic choice. Compare the old site's extra carrier supply, energy and freight with finding, installing and powering a new site's line. A new site's finite ordinary reserve may be a major benefit, but its actual material output and useful stock must be compared rather than assuming every crater is interchangeable.

An illustrative carrier-equivalent comparison: an old site requiring 750 mB fresh per batch versus a new fracking site requiring 250 saves 500 mB per batch. If moving is valued by the player as 50,000 mB carrier-equivalent of effort and expense, its simple payback is 100 equal-output batches. This is not a physical relocation recipe or a universal currency. Travel time, material composition, local power and initial reserve can dominate the result.

### Three Genuine Tradeoffs

1. Higher recovery versus simpler battery-powered production. Powered cleanup and dross recovery reduce freight and bulk feed, but add EU, machine-seconds and buffer stock. Cheap local iron can make the simple alloy route rational. A constrained cargo route can make recovery rational. Better yield need not dominate every site.
2. Strong relocation pressure versus useful permanent infrastructure. Linear deterioration quickly rewards expansion, but punishes investment in an old processing base. Logarithmic deterioration lets that base remain productive, but may not motivate moving when reagents are easy to supply. Tune the fresh-demand horizon against useful output and actual logistics, not merely elapsed hours.
3. Major rare-metal alternative versus trace-additive recovery. A gold/cobalt-heavy alternate recipe can reward a lucky deposit and simplify its local chain. A common-base recovery ladder is easier to understand as cheap construction and produces a clearer 50/75/90 utilization story. Trace reagents still create real cumulative demand and could be expensive. They must stay visible rather than being presented as free catalysts.

### Open Owner Choices And Evidence Limits

- Select which carrier recovery setting, curve shape and fresh-demand horizon are enjoyable. Existing 1,000/750 values are examples only.
- Select whether the three alloy bands should mean different feedstock compositions, progressively better recovery of a common base, or explicitly named alternative routes. The common-base recovery ladder is the economic recommendation, not an accepted replacement for the requested composition bands.
- Select the actual bulk construction uses and consumption scale. Without that demand, 18.52 Au per 1,000 alloy cannot be judged cheap or expensive in practice.
- Compare complete recipe power and machine availability, including normal metal preparation and acid/additive supply. Exact pack access is outside this economics task.
- Verify chemistry separately. Proposed Au/Co/Pt consumption does not establish a real catalytic role, and no precious reagent is returned in these examples.

### Aim And Constraints

The aim is for each composition to have a useful situation. It does not require every player to manufacture all three. A player with a particular base, stocks and transport network can rationally choose one route for a long time. The balance failure is a route that becomes cheaper, faster and easier everywhere once available, leaving the other routes as temporary unlock history rather than useful alternatives.

### Simple Break-Even Tests

Let f, s, a, c, l and d be local marginal acquisition costs per full Fe, Si, Au, Co, Al and Sc unit. They include the actual retained refining chain and the opportunity cost of using that stock here rather than elsewhere. They are not assumed global exchange prices.

Let hF, hG and hS be additional operating costs per finished SGA for each route, including power, consumed reagents and ongoing freight where those are expressed in the same comparison units. A limited machine slot or charging line can also have an opportunity cost. Do not add time as money unless that tradeoff actually matters to the player. Keep EU, machine-seconds and required supply rates visible separately.

| Route | Marginal cost per finished SGA |
| --- | --- |
| Fe/Si | 5f + 0.5s + hF |
| Au/Co | 1.25a + 0.5c + hG |
| Al/Sc | (10l + d)/11 + hS |

Au/Co beats Fe/Si only when:

`a < 4f + 0.4s - 0.4c + 0.8(hF - hG)`

Al/Sc beats Fe/Si only when:

`d < 55f + 5.5s - 10l + 11(hF - hS)`

Al/Sc beats Au/Co only when:

`d < 13.75a + 5.5c - 10l + 11(hG - hS)`

Equality is a tie on these priced axes. Other constraints can decide the choice. Setup capital adds `I/Q` per SGA when a new line costing I is built for Q useful output. Existing installed capital is sunk, but operating it still consumes power, stock and capacity. Avoid charging an already built alternative its original construction cost again while treating the preferred route's construction as free.

### Why The Illustrative Middle Route Is Vulnerable

The Au/Co recipe consumes substantial precious feedstock: 1.25 Au per SGA. Its output is larger than Fe/Si per batch, but that is not an acquisition-cost argument. With equal operating costs and f = s = c = 1, Au must cost less than 4 per unit to beat Fe/Si. If Co alone costs more than 10f + s, Au/Co cannot beat Fe/Si even with free Au. This is a mathematical consequence of the example ratios, not a statement about actual GT prices.

The Al/Sc recipe is a strong competitor because one Sc supports eleven SGA. With f = s = l = 1 and equal operating costs, Sc may cost up to 50.5 per unit before Fe/Si becomes cheaper. Mild Sc scarcity may therefore be insufficient. A later recipe with an easy Sc source could replace both earlier recipes despite using an element called rare.

The middle route needs an honest niche such as a nearby useful Au/Co deposit, joint-product surplus, a constrained Fe supply, a costly Sc supply or a favorable machine/power path. That niche should survive comparison against both alternatives. If actual plausible costs never satisfy those inequalities, change the illustrative ratios or supported production costs rather than disguising the problem with an extra mandatory process.

### Why Competing Uses Or Later Access Are Not Enough

A competing use creates a lasting tradeoff only when the Sc supply or its continuing marginal cost matters relative to combined demand. A fixed one-time recipe sink eventually finishes. Stockpiles then refill. Even a recurring sink may simply cause a player to expand production if its inputs and power are cheap. Do not invent recurring consumption solely to defend another alloy recipe.

An ordinary Al byproduct chain deserves particular caution. The illustrative Al/Sc alloy needs one Sc for every ten Al. If broad Al processing returns at least 0.1 useful Sc per Al at low extra marginal cost, scaling the host chain can supply its own alloy Sc requirement. That would make Sc availability closely follow Al availability rather than creating an independent scarce input. This is a supply-ratio warning, not a claim about actual Bauxite Slag content or a recipe proposal.

At a lower byproduct ratio, the plant must process more host feed for each Sc, creating surplus Al and more power or equipment demand. Those are real costs only if they are not cheaply absorbed by other useful production. Joint-product credits and bottlenecks must be counted honestly. No default cheap Sc recovery from ordinary Bauxite Slag is assumed.

A later destination can delay Al/Sc but does not change its eventual operating economics by itself. Once supply is automated, initial scouting and factory construction become sunk costs. Setup amortization approaches zero per unit as total output grows. If the Al/Sc route then wins on ingredients, EU and sustained throughput everywhere, earlier routes are obsolete in steady state. A long travel distance is only a durable disadvantage if ongoing freight, charging, supply variation or throughput actually matters.

### Output, Power, Throughput And Scale

At identical recipe duration and machine count, the example outputs make Au/Co twice as fast as Fe/Si and Al/Sc 5.5 times as fast as Fe/Si. Al/Sc is also 2.75 times as fast as Au/Co. At identical EU per batch, its EU per SGA has the same advantage. Identical processing settings therefore reinforce the high-output route. Meaningful differences in actual material preparation and operating costs can offset it, but should be established rather than silently assumed.

If recipe times are tF, tG and tS, one machine's final-stage throughputs are 2/tF, 4/tG and 11/tS. Complete-line throughput is instead the smallest relevant supply or machine capacity. If Rm denotes a sustained full-dust supply rate, ingredient supply bounds are:

- Fe/Si: at most the smaller of RFe/5 and 2RSi
- Au/Co: at most the smaller of 0.8RAu and 2RCo
- Al/Sc: at most the smaller of 1.1RAl and 11RSc

These are physical recipe-consumption bounds, not artificial quotas. A scarce Sc supply can make Al/Sc useful for part of a project while Fe/Si or Au/Co fills the remaining demand. No requirement to mix routes is needed. If demand lies below the cheaper route's sustainable supply, using only that route is sensible.

More machines alleviate machine-seconds and peak throughput, but not consumed EU per SGA or reagent quantity. They also need power and capital. During the provisional surface-charged battery bootstrap, extra refining EU becomes extra charging and transported energy. Cheap later non-combustion power may change that comparison. No combustion exception or placeable Galaxia solar generator is assumed.

Small projects favor existing nearby machinery and available stock. Large automated projects amortize setup and expose the true continuing costs. Route diversity that exists only because an extra machine is initially expensive may disappear once all lines are built. The same applies to one-time recipe discovery or unlock work.

### Crater Deterioration Can Supply A Real Tradeoff

The profile determines how much useful normal material a fixed enriched batch eventually supplies through retained refining. Carrier demand for an old profile rises while its enriched output stays fixed. Cost per useful dust can therefore rise even when output per operation does not fall. No comparison should assume an Fe-rich and an Sc-rich profile yield identical numbers of finished metal units.

Several machines on one crater advance its shared cost sequence faster. Players may shift extraction to new sites, but then face real installation, power and freight costs. There is no hard cap or invented Sc production quota. Profile-dependent cost growth can alter local marginal acquisition prices and help preserve situational alternatives, but it does not prove diversity by itself. Easily accessible new Sc sites or an abundant broad byproduct route can still keep Sc cheap.

Finite ordinary reserves offer temporary cheap stock before fracking. Balance needs to check both that opening phase and the ongoing fracking supply. A recipe justified only by a lucky first reserve can remain a useful opportunistic recipe, but it should not be presented as the stable cheapest mass-production route.

### Recommendation And Remaining Evidence

Prefer A as the simplest fit for the existing crater system if the material evidence supports a useful limited Sc-bearing supply. That creates local price and capacity differences without adding new machinery solely to preserve recipe diversity. Keep actual competing uses visible, but do not rely on them alone.

If evidence instead supports broad Sc-bearing feed, C is the cleaner alternative. Count its justified continuing extraction/refining burden explicitly. Do not manufacture geographic scarcity merely to force the answer. B can be an additional location choice or a content decision, but should not be the sole economic defense of earlier routes. Even with B, test post-access steady-state costs and freight.

Keep the Fe route useful and examine the Au/Co ratio carefully. The middle route has a plausible surplus-site niche, but the illustrative recipe consumes a lot of Au. If plausible supported supplies never make it rational, adjust those provisional quantities or choose a genuinely different operating-cost advantage. Do not force all three routes through a quota, mandated alternating craft or extra complexity.

The main remaining evidence is actual Sc location and separation feasibility, retained GT process availability, local material supply rates, complete per-SGA EU and machine-seconds, and realistic transport cost after automation.
